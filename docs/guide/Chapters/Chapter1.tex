\section{What this part covers}

This guide grows with the code. Part~I covers the two modules of \code{fluxlb.core} that
are complete and tested, the device and precision utilities in \file{gpu.py} and the
velocity sets in \file{lattices.py}, together with the tests that verify them. It stops
where the solver does: streaming, collision, boundary conditions and the time loop are not
yet implemented and are not described here.

\section{Notation}

A lattice has $Q$ discrete velocities $\vc_i$, $i = 0,\dots,Q-1$, in $D$ dimensions, with
weights $w_i$ and squared lattice sound speed $\cs^2$. The distribution tensor is $f$ with
shape $[Q, *\text{spatial}]$, so that index~0 runs over velocities. The opposite direction
of $i$ is $\bar{i}$, with $\vc_{\bar{i}} = -\vc_i$. Greek indices label spatial components
and $\delta_{\alpha\beta}$ is the Kronecker delta. The weighted lattice sum is
$\langle g \rangle = \sum_i w_i\, g(\vc_i)$.

\section{Design principles carried by these modules}

Two conventions from \file{CLAUDE.md} shape everything in this part.

\begin{description}[style=nextline]
\item[One device and dtype context.]
Lattice constants are registered as PyTorch buffers so that a single
\code{module.to(device, dtype)} moves the whole solver. Nothing in a hot loop may query the
device or copy data to the host.

\item[fp32 compute, fp64 accumulation.]
Populations and collisions are computed in fp32. Conserved moments (density and momentum)
are accumulated in fp64 where the hardware allows it. The primary node has weak fp64
throughput and Apple's MPS backend has no fp64 at all, so the accumulation dtype is a
per-device decision made in one place (\cref{sec:precision}).
\end{description}

\begin{draft}[why these two conventions follow from the physics and the hardware]
In the weakly compressible regime the lattice Boltzmann method simulates, the pressure is
$p = \cs^2\rho$ and the density varies by $O(\mathrm{Ma}^2)$ about its mean. The physically
meaningful signal in $\rho = \sum_i f_i$ is therefore a small deviation of a sum of $Q$
terms of order one, and the velocity $\vu = \sum_i \vc_i f_i / \rho$ divides one such sum by
another. A reduction of $Q$ terms in floating point with unit round-off $\varepsilon$ carries
an error of order $Q\varepsilon$ relative to the terms \parencite{Higham2002}: about
$10^{-6}$ in fp32 for $Q = 9$, which is comparable to the density deviation at
$\mathrm{Ma} \approx 10^{-3}$ and is incurred afresh at every node and every time step.
Because collision recomputes the equilibrium from $\rho$ and $\vu$, that rounding feeds
straight back into the populations, and mass and momentum drift as a random walk over the
run. Accumulating the moments in fp64 makes the reduction error $Q\varepsilon_{64} \sim
10^{-15}$, negligible against the fp32 representation of the populations themselves, so the
conservation error is set by the storage precision alone. The population update, by
contrast, is a local relaxation whose $O(\varepsilon_{32})$ rounding does not accumulate in
the same way and whose cost dominates the step, so fp32 there is the right trade on hardware
with weak fp64.

On MPS there is no fp64, and the accumulation falls back to fp32 with a warning. The
conservation tests must then tolerate a per-step mass error of order $Q\varepsilon_{32}
\approx 10^{-6}$ instead of $10^{-15}$, and long runs on MPS should be treated as
development runs, not as reference results. That is the reason the fallback warns rather
than fails.
\end{draft}
